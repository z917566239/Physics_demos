Alice sends single photons, each polarized in one of four directions. Bob picks a filter at random to measure each one. Where their filters match, they now share a bit. A spy has to guess filters too, and wrong guesses leave errors behind.

Try it: set both filters to + and every bit matches. Set them different and Bob gets coin flips. Then turn on Eve and send 100.

  • Same filter, same bitBob reads Alice's bit perfectly when he happens to use her filter. That happens about half the time.
  • Filters are compared in publicAfterwards they announce which filter they used (never the bit) and keep only the matches.
  • Spying leaves fingerprintsA single photon can't be copied. Eve must measure and resend, and her wrong guesses push errors to about 25%.

Glass fibre swallows a little light every kilometre, so over long distances almost nothing gets through. From a satellite, photons fly through empty space and only meet thick air in the last few kilometres. That air is turbulent, and how much it hurts depends on its r₀ and on which way the beam goes.

  • Space is kind to photonsVacuum absorbs nothing. Most of the loss is the beam spreading out wider than the 30 cm telescope that catches it.
  • Low in the sky is harderNear the horizon the satellite is farther away, the light crosses much more air, and r₀ shrinks.
  • Direction mattersBeaming down, turbulence blurs the image at the telescope. Beaming up, it bends the beam right at the start, so it spreads and wanders by the time it reaches orbit.

Air wrinkles the light

stillgusty
Flatness ruler
500 nm

Warm and cool pockets of air bend light by slightly different amounts. A flat wavefront goes in, a wrinkled one comes out, and the telescope can no longer focus it to one sharp point. The wrinkles are drawn to scale: the gap between lines is one wavelength. Change the wavelength and the air's bumps stay the same size, but they become a smaller fraction of each wave.

  • Flat, but only up closeLay a ruler r₀ wide on the wrinkled wavefront and it hugs a straight line. Make the ruler 3 × r₀ and it clearly doesn't. That is what r₀ means.
  • Wrinkles tilt the lightEach r₀-sized piece of the wavefront points a slightly different way, so the rays no longer meet at one spot.
  • Wind makes it danceThe air drifts past, so the blur jumps around many times a second. That's why stars twinkle.
  • Longer waves shrug it offAir delays every wavelength by almost the same distance. For a longer wave that delay is a smaller slice of the wave, so r₀ grows: three times the wavelength gives almost four times the r₀.

r₀ (the Fried parameter) is the width of a patch where the wrinkled wavefront still looks flat. Big r₀ means calm air. Small r₀ means strong turbulence. A telescope wider than r₀ sees many patches at once, and its image breaks up.

  • Smaller than r₀: sharp but jumpyA small telescope sees about one flat patch. The spot stays sharp, it just wanders as the patch tilts.
  • Bigger than r₀: specklesA big telescope sees many patches, each sending light a slightly different way. The spot splits into speckles.
  • Removing tip-tilt helps, up to a pointA fast steering mirror cancels the overall tilt and steadies the spot. Once the mirror spans many r₀, the speckles remain; fixing those needs adaptive optics.
  • Longer wavelength: bigger r₀, bigger ideal spotAt 1550 nm r₀ is almost four times larger than at 500 nm, but the perfect-air spot also grows three times wider. The blur from the air shrinks only a little.
